# Why short model-written prose can be hard to understand: research notes

These notes collect primary evidence on one question: why can engineering prose written by a language model be easy to skim but hard to understand on a careful reading, and which writing rules reduce that cost? Every claim below names its source. When a source could not be opened, the note says so and names where the claim came from instead. Sources were retrieved on 2026-09-27; the Anthropic documentation in particular changes often, so recheck quoted wording before relying on it.

## 1. Summary

The strongest explanation is that the writer cannot judge what the reader is missing: people who hold information systematically overestimate how well others will understand them, and asking them to try harder does not fix the error (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber 1989; Newton 1990; Hinds 1999; Keysar & Henly 2002; Kruger et al. 2005). Text-comprehension research then shows what the missing pieces cost, because readers who lack background knowledge understand less when a text leaves out the links between its claims, and even most expert readers do better when the links are stated (McNamara et al. 1996; O'Reilly & McNamara 2007; Ozuru, Dempsey & McNamara 2009). Causal and contrastive connectives such as "because" and "but" measurably help comprehension, while telegraphic text that drops function words is read faster but understood less (Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders 2019; Degand & Sanders 2002; Martin & Herndon 1972). An agentic model adds a further gap, because it has read tool output and files that the user never saw, and Anthropic's own documentation tells developers to make the model restate such output and close with a recap that stands on its own. The evidence therefore supports short sentences and cutting padding, but it argues against deleting reasons, connectives and grammar, and against letting the writer decide alone what the reader can do without.

## 2. Evidence by hypothesis

### Hypothesis 1: the writer holds the context, so bridging information looks redundant and gets cut

**Verdict: supported for human writers; partly supported for language models.** The human evidence is direct and consistent. The model-side evidence comes from two recent studies of related tasks, not from a study of model-written explanations.

**Evidence**

- **Economic forecasting (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber 1989).** The authors define the "curse of knowledge" as a case where "better-informed agents are unable to ignore private information even when it is in their interest to do so" (abstract, opened as the author-hosted PDF). In the design, 51 Wharton students first predicted eight companies' 1980 earnings from analyst reports. A second group, who were told the actual earnings, then had to predict the first group's forecasts, and their predictions drifted toward the true figures that the first group never had. Market trading reduced this bias "by approximately 50 percent but [did] not eliminate it" (abstract). Population and scope: business students predicting numbers, with money at stake. The gap for this question is large, because the task was numeric prediction rather than writing, but the mechanism is the same: knowing more made it harder to model someone who knows less.
- **Tapping songs (Newton 1990, Stanford PhD dissertation).** In Part I, 80 Stanford undergraduates worked in pairs; "tappers" finger-tapped well-known tunes and estimated how often a listener would name them. Tappers' estimates averaged 50%, but listeners succeeded on "only 3 hits in 120 tries, a success rate of only 2.5 percent", which was "outside the entire range of the tapper's estimates" (dissertation body, opened as a scanned copy hosted at gwern.net). The dissertation abstract gives a slightly different count ("two out of one hundred fifty tunes"), so the exact figure is inconsistent inside the source. Informed observers who knew the tune but never tapped it were also overconfident. Note that popular retellings describe the participants as graduate students, but the dissertation says undergraduates. The gap: the signal was rhythm, not language, but it shows that the sender's knowledge fills in what the signal lacks.
- **Ambiguous sentences (Keysar & Henly 2002, *Psychological Science*).** Speakers who said ambiguous sentences while intending one meaning "showed a consistent tendency to overestimate their effectiveness", whereas informed overhearers did not; the authors conclude that speakers "underestimate the ambiguity of their own utterances" (PubMed abstract, PMID 12009039). Population: adult lab participants speaking single sentences. The gap is that this is speech, not written technical prose, but the result applies directly to a writer rereading its own sentence and judging it clear.
- **Email (Kruger, Epley, Parker & Ng 2005, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*).** Across five experiments, people believed they could convey tone over email better than they could, and Studies 4 and 5 trace this overconfidence to egocentrism, "the inherent difficulty of detaching oneself from one's own perspective" (PubMed abstract, PMID 16393025). The gap: the study measured tone such as sarcasm, not technical content, but the medium is written text without a live listener, as in chat.
- **Experts predicting novices (Hinds 1999, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*).** "Those with more expertise were worse predictors of novice performance times and were resistant to debiasing techniques intended to reduce underestimation", and people with intermediate expertise predicted more accurately (abstract, read through the OpenAlex record because the publisher page was blocked; full text not opened). The detail that the task involved learning a mobile phone comes from a secondary source (a *Psychology Today* blog post) and was not verified. The gap: the study measured time predictions, not writing, but the failure of debiasing matters for any rule that asks the writer to check itself.
- **Writing advice (Pinker 2014, "The Source of Bad Writing", *Wall Street Journal*).** Pinker defines the curse of knowledge as "a difficulty in imagining what it is like for someone else not to know something that you know", says the cursed writer "doesn't bother to explain the jargon, or spell out the logic, or supply the necessary detail", and argues that "just trying harder to put yourself in someone else's shoes doesn't make you much more accurate". His remedy is to "show a draft to some people who are similar to your intended audience and find out whether they can follow it". This was read in a reprint on the Center for Plain Language site, which states it is reprinted with the author's permission; the author-hosted PDF is a scan that could not be text-extracted, and the book *The Sense of Style*, chapter 3, was not opened.
- **Language models simulating less-informed users (Ma et al. 2026, arXiv 2608.25245).** When eight language models wrote search queries as if they were users who had not yet seen the answer, some queries contained concepts that only the answer documents supplied, and "under the tested conditions, no prompt condition eliminates intrusion" (arXiv abstract). The gap: this is query writing for search evaluation, not explanation, but it is the closest primary evidence that a model's own knowledge leaks into text meant for someone who lacks it, and that prompting alone did not stop the leak.
- **Language-model agents guiding other agents (Baek, Liu & Ukpong 2025, arXiv 2512.15776).** In a simulated household environment, a model that could see the target guided a model that could not, and the pair succeeded in 17% of episodes although the leader saw the target in 35%; the authors attribute the difference to "communicative grounding errors" and found that follower questions helped (arXiv abstract). The gap: agent-to-agent instructions in a simulator, with no human readers.

**What is missing.** No study was found that measures whether a language model writing explanations for a human omits the reasons and links that the human needs. The hypothesis for models therefore rests on the human evidence plus two indirect model studies.

### Hypothesis 2: missing connectives and links force bridging inferences that some readers cannot make

A "bridging inference" is a reader's own reconstruction of a link that the text leaves unstated, for example working out that one sentence is the cause of the next.

**Verdict: supported, with an important qualification about expert readers.** Low-knowledge readers consistently understand less when links are missing. A minority of high-knowledge readers learn more from a gappy text under specific conditions, but most skilled expert readers still do better with the links stated.

**Evidence**

- **Theory (Kintsch 1988, *Psychological Review*).** The construction-integration model describes comprehension as building a network of propositions and inferences and then integrating it "into a coherent structure through a spreading activation process" (abstract via OpenAlex; full paper not opened). This is the theoretical background for the studies below, not a test of it.
- **Repairing gaps improves learning (Britton & Gülgöz 1991, *Journal of Educational Psychology*).** The authors used Kintsch's model to find places in a 1000-word expository text where readers had to supply an inference, and rewrote each place. "Two experiments with 170 undergraduates and 125 Air Force recruits indicated that the principled revision conveyed the author's intentions better than the original text" (ERIC record EJ436892). A search-engine snippet of the publisher abstract adds that free recall "was much increased" and that the revision produced "more learning per unit of study time"; that wording was not opened at the source. The gap: the text was about an air war, and the readers were students and recruits, not engineers.
- **The interaction with prior knowledge (McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch 1996, *Cognition and Instruction*).** Junior high school students read biology texts that varied in local and global coherence. "Readers who know little about the domain of the text benefit from a coherent text, whereas high-knowledge readers benefit from a minimally coherent text", and the benefit for high-knowledge readers appeared on measures of deeper understanding, not on recall (abstract, read on the Arizona State University research portal). The authors explain that "the poorly written text forces the knowledgeable readers to engage in compensatory processing to infer unstated relations". The literature calls this the "reverse cohesion effect".
- **The effect depends on reading order (McNamara 2001, *Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology*).** High-knowledge readers "benefited from the low-coherence only text when it was read first"; low-knowledge readers "benefited from the high-coherence text, regardless of whether it was read first, second, or twice" (PubMed abstract, PMID 11301728).
- **The effect is limited to less skilled readers (O'Reilly & McNamara 2007, *Discourse Processes*).** With 143 college students, "the benefit of low-cohesion text was restricted to less skilled, high-knowledge readers, whereas skilled comprehenders with high knowledge benefited from a high-cohesion text", and low-knowledge readers gained on bridging-inference questions from the cohesive text (ERIC record EJ772185).
- **Cohesion helps skilled readers most (Ozuru, Dempsey & McNamara 2009, *Learning and Instruction*).** College students read a high-cohesion and a low-cohesion biology text. High cohesion "improved text-based comprehension", and "skilled participants gained more from high-cohesion text" (abstract, Arizona State University research portal).
- **Connectives by type (Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders 2019, *Discourse Processes*).** In 20 authentic Dutch texts read by 794 adolescents, adding connectives improved local comprehension, and the global effect appeared for difficult texts only. "Contrastive and causal connectives increased comprehension, whereas additive connectives reduced comprehension", and the effect did not differ by reading proficiency (abstract via OpenAlex; open-access article not read in full).
- **Causal markers (Degand & Sanders 2002, *Reading and Writing*).** In short expository texts read in Dutch and French as first and second languages, "readers benefit from the presence of causal relational markers both in L1 and in L2" (Springer abstract).
- **Markers speed reading more than they aid memory (Sanders & Noordman 2000, *Discourse Processes*).** Explicitly marking a coherence relation "resulted in faster processing but did not affect recall", whereas the type of relation (problem and solution versus a list) affected both (abstract via OpenAlex).

**The gap for chat-based engineering prose.** These studies used students reading science or expository texts, usually a few hundred to a thousand words. None used developers reading chat replies. The reverse cohesion effect also has a precondition that rarely holds in agentic chat: the reader must already know the missing link. A senior engineer knows the domain, but no reader knows what a tool returned in a session they did not see, so domain expertise cannot fill that kind of gap (this is an inference from McNamara 2001, which ties the effect to "the use of knowledge to fill in the gaps").

### Hypothesis 3: telegraphic compression and dropped function words cost comprehension

"Telegraphic" text drops function words such as articles, auxiliary verbs, prepositions and conjunctions, and it often replaces verbs with arrows, dashes or colons. A "nominalization" is a verb or adjective turned into a noun, such as "implementation" for "implement". A "noun string" or "noun stack" is several nouns in a row, such as "cache invalidation retry policy".

**Verdict: partly supported.** Direct experimental evidence on telegraphic prose is old and thin, but it points the same way as the controlled-language and plain-language standards, which all forbid dropping words to save length. The evidence does not argue against short sentences, which the same standards recommend.

**Evidence**

- **Telegraphic prose experiment (Martin & Herndon 1972, conference paper, ERIC ED062090).** Two experiments with 60 remedial-reading and 100 introductory-course undergraduates compared a 1620-word story with a 455-word telegraphic version. Readers spent "less than one-half the time" on the telegraphic version, "but there was approximately 12 percent less comprehension" (ERIC abstract and full text). Limitations: this is an unreviewed conference paper, the material was a fictional story, and the telegraphic version cut far more than function words.
- **Newspaper headlines (Perfetti et al. 1987, *Journal of Memory and Language*, "Comprehending newspaper headlines").** Not opened; no abstract was available through OpenAlex, so no finding is claimed here.
- **Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100, Issue 9, January 2025).** This controlled-language standard for technical documentation states Rule 4.2, "Do not omit words or use contractions to make your sentences shorter", and explains that "your sentence will be shorter, but it will not be easier to read". It adds "Do not omit verbs because the reader will not understand the action" (example: "Rotary switch to INPUT" becomes "Set the rotary switch to INPUT"), "Do not omit the subject", and "Do not omit articles … because omitted articles can cause ambiguity". Rule 4.5 asks for an article or "this"/"these" before a noun where applicable, Rule 4.4 asks for "connecting words and connecting phrases" between related sentences, and Rule 2.1 limits multi-word nouns to three words because "the words in the multi-word noun can connect to each other differently". The same standard also requires short sentences (Rule 4.1). Source: the specification PDF published at asd-ste100.org. The gap: it is an industry standard built from practice, not an experiment, and it targets maintenance procedures.
- **Federal Plain Language Guidelines (PLAIN, March 2011, revision 1 May 2011).** On noun strings: "Readability suffers when three words that are ordinarily separate nouns follow in succession", because "many users will think they've found the noun when they're still reading adjectives"; the fix is "using more prepositions and articles to clarify the relationships among the words". On hidden verbs (nominalizations): "Use the strongest, most direct form of the verb possible." On connectives: "more often we simply overlook using them. Being too familiar with our own material, we think they aren't needed. Readers, on the other hand, find them helpful." Source: the guideline PDF, opened through the Internet Archive copy of plainlanguage.gov, because plainlanguage.gov now redirects to a shorter guide on digital.gov that still keeps the hidden-verbs guidance.
- **Nominalizations and recall (Spyridakis & Isakson 1998, *Journal of Technical Writing and Communication*).** The study found that "denominalized text is most effective in helping native speakers focus on more important information, but for nonnative speakers, nominalized text may not work well" (ERIC record EJ569911; full text not opened).
- **GOV.UK guidance.** The "Use clear language" page says "Words ending in '–ion' and '–ment' tend to make sentences longer and more complicated than they need to be", asks writers to "split up sentences that are over 25 words long", and says "If you write only a single paragraph but it's full of jargon, then it's too long" (guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk). The same page claims that "the more educated the person and the more specialist their knowledge, the greater their preference for plain English", citing unnamed research on legal documents; that underlying study was not identified or opened.
- **Helen Sword, "Zombie Nouns" (*New York Times* Opinionator, 23 July 2012).** Sword argues that nominalizations "cannibalize active verbs" and "substitute abstract entities for human beings", so a sentence full of them "fails to tell us who is doing what" (column opened on archive.nytimes.com). This is an essay by the author, not an experiment.

**The gap.** No study was found that tests arrow-and-dash notation or clipped chat fragments on developers. The case against them rests on one old experiment plus the standards above.

### Hypothesis 4: an agent refers to tool output and working labels the user never saw

**Verdict: supported by Anthropic's own documentation and by research on conversational grounding.** No experiment on users of agentic coding tools was found.

**Evidence from Anthropic's documentation (retrieved 2026-09-27)**

- **Prompting best practices** (https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices), section "Communication style and verbosity": "Claude's latest models have a more concise and natural communication style compared to previous models", including "**Less verbose:** May skip detailed summaries for efficiency unless prompted otherwise", so "Claude may skip verbal summaries after tool calls, jumping directly to the next action." The suggested fix is the prompt "After completing a task that involves tool use, provide a quick summary of the work you've done." The same page notes that Claude Opus 5 is an exception whose default responses "run longer than prior models'".
- **The same page, on formatting**, offers a sample instruction asking for "clear, flowing prose using complete paragraphs and sentences", says "This guidance applies especially to technical writing", and states the goal as "readable, flowing text that guides the reader naturally through ideas rather than fragmenting information into isolated points."
- **The same page, on prompts written for Claude**: "Think of Claude as a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows", with the "golden rule" to "Show your prompt to a colleague with minimal context on the task". This advice is about prompts that humans write for the model, not about the model's replies, so applying it to replies is an analogy and not Anthropic's claim.
- **Prompting Claude Fable 5.1** (https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-1), section "Ask for user-facing progress updates": the model "write[s] fewer user-facing updates during long tool-calling turns", and users may see "a final message that covers only the last step rather than the whole task." The recommended system-prompt line asks the model to "Close with a short recap that stands on its own — what you found, what you did, and what's next — so a reader who only sees the last message has the full picture." For products that hide tool output, the page recommends telling the model: "Only you see that command's output — the user's terminal shows at most a few lines of it. If the user needs to read any of it, put it in your reply."
- **The same page, section "Writing density"**: the model's "prose is denser than Claude Fable 5's: sentences run longer and there are fewer paragraph breaks", and Anthropic supplies an instruction against "mannered prose", which "substitutes metaphor and flourish for direct statement" and "makes the reader work harder so the writer can perform"; it ends "When a literal phrase is available, use it."
- **Prompting Claude Opus 5** (https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-5) recommends, for progress updates, "When you finish, lead with the outcome: your first sentence should answer 'what happened' or 'what did you find,' with supporting detail after it for readers who want it."
- **Claude Code output styles** (https://code.claude.com/docs/en/output-styles): the built-in "Concise" style means "the first sentence of a response states what happened or what the answer is" and Claude "leaves out the lead-in, the step-by-step narration, and the closing recap". The "Explanatory" style instead "adds short explanations of why it made the choices it made". Anthropic therefore treats the reasons behind a change as optional content that some users want, which is compatible with this hypothesis but does not test it.

**Evidence from grounding research**

- **Overhearers understand less than addressees (Schober & Clark 1989, *Cognitive Psychology*).** Speakers design utterances "against the common ground they share with their addressees". In a figure-arranging task, "addressees were more accurate at arranging the figures than overhearers even when the overhearers heard every word", because addressees could collaborate to confirm understanding (abstract via OpenAlex). A user who reads an agent's summary of work they did not watch is in a position similar to the overhearer's.
- **References are negotiated (Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs 1986, *Cognition*).** Speaker and addressee "repair, expand on, or replace the noun phrase in an iterative process until they reach a version they mutually accept" (abstract via OpenAlex). A label the model invented while working alone has not been through this process with the user.

**The gap.** The grounding studies used spoken, live tasks with strangers. The Anthropic pages are guidance for developers, not studies, and they describe specific model versions that will change.

### Hypothesis 5: model-side drivers such as training pressure and brevity instructions

**Verdict: partly supported, and one common assumption is refuted.** Preference training pushes models toward longer answers, not shorter ones, so terseness is more likely to come from explicit brevity instructions and from newer model defaults. No primary study was found that measures how well humans understand model prose written under brevity instructions.

**Evidence**

- **Preference training rewards length (Singhal, Goyal, Xu & Durrett 2023, arXiv 2310.03716).** "RLHF also often drives models to produce longer outputs", and "even a purely length-based reward reproduces most downstream RLHF improvements"; the source of the bias is mainly the reward models (arXiv abstract).
- **The same bias in direct preference optimization (Park, Rafailov, Ermon & Finn 2024, arXiv 2403.19159).** "RLHF is know[n] to exploit biases in human preferences, such as verbosity. A well-formatted and eloquent answer is often more highly rated by users, even when it is less helpful" (arXiv abstract).
- **Model judges prefer length (Saito et al. 2023, arXiv 2310.10076; Dubois et al. 2024, arXiv 2404.04475).** GPT-4 as a judge "prefers longer answers more than humans", and the AlpacaEval benchmark needed a length correction (arXiv abstracts).
- **Over-long answers on simple requests (Borisov et al. 2026, arXiv 2601.00624).** Across 76 assistant models, the authors found "redundant explanations, hedging, or boilerplate" on prompts where a short answer is ideal (arXiv abstract). This supports cutting padding; it does not measure comprehension.
- **Brevity and model accuracy (Renze & Guven 2024, arXiv 2401.05618; Nayab et al. 2024, arXiv 2407.19825; Zahedzadeh & Bahrak 2026, arXiv 2602.14002).** Concise reasoning prompts cut response length by about half with "negligible impact" on multiple-choice accuracy, but GPT-3.5 lost 27.69% on math (Renze & Guven); "excessive compression leads to performance degradation" (Zahedzadeh & Bahrak). These papers measure whether the model still reaches the right answer, not whether a human reader understands the text, so they do not test this question.
- **Reader-perceived clarity is not comprehension (Moraru, Biswas & Gadiraju 2026, arXiv 2608.20896).** With 103 crowd workers, model-rewritten Python error messages "significantly improved subjective evaluations", but "these perceived gains did not translate into statistically significant improvements in objective debugging performance" (arXiv abstract). This matters for how a writing rule is evaluated: a reader saying text feels easier is weak evidence that it is easier to understand.
- **Developers miss errors in fluent answers (Kabir et al. 2024, arXiv 2308.02312).** Of 517 programming answers, 52% contained incorrect information and 77% were verbose; user-study participants still preferred them 35% of the time and "overlooked the misinformation" 39% of the time (arXiv abstract).
- **Anthropic's description of current defaults** (see Hypothesis 4): recent models are "less verbose" and "may skip verbal summaries after tool calls", while Claude Opus 5 runs longer by default. Terseness is therefore partly a documented default of some model versions, and not only a result of user instructions.

**The gap.** No study was found that asks humans to read model explanations produced with and without brevity instructions and then tests their understanding.

### Hypothesis 6: coined terms and undefined labels the reader cannot look up

**Verdict: supported by accessibility and plain-language guidance and by research on how conversational partners agree on names.** No study of model-coined labels was found.

**Evidence**

- **W3C, "Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities" (Working Group Note, 29 April 2021), pattern 4.4.1 "Use Clear Words".** "Remove or explain uncommon acronyms, abbreviations, and jargon", and "Do not invent new words or give words new meanings in your application. Do not expect people to learn new meanings for words just to use your content. If you must create new terms, make sure the user has access to an explanation within one click or event." Source: https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/.
- **The same document, pattern 4.4.4 "Use Literal Language"**: "Use literal and concrete language" and "Do not use metaphors and similes unless you include an explanation."
- **The same document, pattern 4.4.12 "Explain Implied Content"**: provide explanations for implied or ambiguous information "in text close to the implied content", because implied content "requires the user to have additional knowledge to understand."
- **The same document, pattern 4.4.6 "Use Clear, Unambiguous Formatting and Punctuation"**: "Avoid the use of Roman Numerals and unfamiliar symbols in text where possible", partly because text-to-speech may misread them; it also notes that a hyphen "will generally be ignored" by speech output. This is relevant to arrows and dashes that stand in for verbs.
- **The same document, pattern 4.4.5 "Keep Text Succinct"**, supports short sentences with "only one point per sentence", but it also advises: "Double-check if a long sentence is clearer than two short sentences. Do usability testing."
- **Federal Plain Language Guidelines (2011), "Minimize abbreviations".** Abbreviations "constantly require the reader to look back"; the preferred fix is a descriptive "nickname" such as "the committee", and a document should use "no more than three, and preferably two" abbreviations.
- **GOV.UK "Use clear language".** Specialist terms "are not always jargon. You just need to explain what they mean the first time you use them."
- **Names are agreed between partners (Brennan & Clark 1996, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*).** When people refer repeatedly to an object, they form "partner-specific conceptualizations", called "conceptual pacts", and "Over time, speakers simplify conceptual pacts" (PubMed abstract, PMID 8921603). A label that a model coins and shortens during a working session is a name agreed with nobody, because the user was not a partner in forming it.
- **Jargon impairs processing (Bullock, Colón Amill, Shulman & Dixon 2019, *Public Understanding of Science*).** In an experiment with 650 participants, "the presence of jargon impairs people's ability to process scientific information" (PubMed abstract, PMID 31354058). A follow-up found no effect of jargon for the urgent COVID-19 topic while it still hurt processing for less urgent topics (Shulman & Bullock 2020, *PLoS ONE*), so motivated readers may tolerate it. The gap: these participants were members of the public reading about science, not engineers reading about their own code, and neither study tested invented labels.

## 3. Rules this evidence supports

Each rule is written so that a reviewer can check a reply against it. The examples use an invented library-catalog service.

1. **Keep causal and contrastive connectives.** When a sentence states a reason, a result or an exception, keep "because", "so", "but" or "although". Source: Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders 2019; Degand & Sanders 2002; ASD-STE100 Rule 4.4; Federal Plain Language Guidelines, "Use transition words". Check: every recommendation or claim of cause has its connective. Example: write "The catalog caches search results for five minutes, so a newly added book may not appear right away", not "Cache TTL 5 min — new books delayed."
2. **Do not add additive connectives for their own sake.** Words such as "also" and "in addition" did not help and reduced comprehension in the largest study. Source: Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders 2019. Check: additive connectives appear only where they mark a real addition.
3. **State the reason for a recommendation in the same sentence or the next one.** Gaps that the reader must fill by inference reduce what readers without the background understand. Source: Britton & Gülgöz 1991; McNamara et al. 1996; Ozuru, Dempsey & McNamara 2009. Check: no recommendation stands without a stated reason nearby.
4. **Keep articles, verbs and subjects.** Do not let an arrow, a dash or a colon stand in for a verb. Source: ASD-STE100 Rules 4.2 and 4.5; Martin & Herndon 1972; W3C COGA pattern 4.4.6. Check: every line that states a fact contains a finite verb. Example: write "The renewal job sends a reminder three days before the due date", not "Renewal job → reminder, due −3d."
5. **Split long sentences without losing the link.** Short sentences are well supported, but when one sentence becomes two, carry the relation into the second one with "so", "because of this" or "in this case". Source: Federal Plain Language Guidelines, "Write short sentences" (its own example keeps the "if … then" relation by starting sentences with "Suppose" and "In this case"); GOV.UK 25-word guidance; W3C COGA pattern 4.4.5.
6. **Limit noun stacks to three words.** Open longer ones with prepositions and articles. Source: ASD-STE100 Rule 2.1; Federal Plain Language Guidelines, noun strings. Example: write "the policy for retrying failed holds", not "hold failure retry policy".
7. **Prefer verbs to nominalizations.** Source: Federal Plain Language Guidelines, hidden verbs; GOV.UK "Use clear language"; Spyridakis & Isakson 1998; Sword 2012. Example: write "the service validates the barcode", not "barcode validation occurs".
8. **Restate what the reader did not see.** Do not refer to tool output, file contents or earlier reasoning as if the reader saw it; quote or summarize the relevant part in the reply. Source: Anthropic, "Prompting Claude Fable 5.1" ("If the user needs to read any of it, put it in your reply"); Schober & Clark 1989. Check: every "the error", "the fix" or "that test" has an antecedent visible in the reply or in the user's own messages. Example: write "The test for overdue fines fails because it expects 0.50 but the function returns 0.5", not "Fixed the mismatch in the fines test."
9. **End work with a recap that stands on its own.** Say what was found, what was done and what is next, so that a reader who sees only the last message understands the state. Source: Anthropic, "Prompting Claude Fable 5.1"; Anthropic, "Prompting Claude Opus 5" (lead with the outcome).
10. **Define or avoid labels the reader has not seen.** Use the plain descriptive phrase instead of a coined compound, and do not give an existing word a new meaning. If a new term is necessary, define it at first use. Source: W3C COGA pattern 4.4.1; Brennan & Clark 1996; Federal Plain Language Guidelines, abbreviations; GOV.UK technical terms. Check: every term that is not standard in the domain and did not come from the user is defined where it first appears. Example: write "the step that merges duplicate author records", not "the dedupe pass" when that name was invented during the session.
11. **Use literal language.** Replace metaphors with the direct statement. Source: W3C COGA pattern 4.4.4; Anthropic's "mannered prose" instruction for Claude Fable 5.1.
12. **Explain necessary technical terms instead of deleting them.** Source: GOV.UK "Use clear language"; Federal Plain Language Guidelines, jargon section ("we're not advocating leaving out necessary technical terms").
13. **Do not rely on the writer's own sense of what is obvious.** Use checks that do not depend on the writer's judgment, such as rules 1, 3, 4, 8 and 10 above, or a test with a reader who lacks the context. Source: Hinds 1999 (debiasing failed); Keysar & Henly 2002; Pinker 2014; Ma et al. 2026 (no prompt condition removed the intrusion).
14. **Evaluate rules with comprehension tests, not with how easy the text feels.** Faster reading and higher clarity ratings can come with lower comprehension. Source: Martin & Herndon 1972; Sanders & Noordman 2000; Moraru, Biswas & Gadiraju 2026.

## 4. Rules this evidence argues against

1. **"One clause where possible", when it removes the clause that carries a reason.** Subordinate clauses introduced by "because", "so that" or "although" are where causal and contrastive relations usually live, and those relations are the ones shown to help comprehension (Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders 2019; Degand & Sanders 2002). The evidence does not oppose short sentences, which ASD-STE100, GOV.UK, the Federal Plain Language Guidelines and W3C COGA all recommend. It opposes shortening that drops the relation instead of moving it into the next sentence.
2. **A deletion test judged by the writer.** A test such as "does removing this cost the reader anything?" asks the writer to predict the reader's state, which is the judgment the curse-of-knowledge studies show to be biased and resistant to correction (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber 1989; Hinds 1999; Keysar & Henly 2002; Kruger et al. 2005). The model studies suggest the same bias survives prompting (Ma et al. 2026).
3. **Worked examples that compress a reason into a label and a dash.** Rewriting "the catalog locks a record while a librarian edits it, so two people cannot overwrite each other's changes" as "Edit lock — one editor per record" removes the verb, the subject and the consequence. It conflicts with ASD-STE100 Rules 4.2 and 4.4, and it also conflicts with the principle of cutting content rather than grammar. Examples teach more strongly than rules, so an example like this can override a correct rule stated next to it.
4. **Including reasons and caveats only when the writer judges them decision-relevant.** The reason behind a recommendation is what lets a careful reader build an understanding of the situation rather than a list of claims (McNamara et al. 1996, which found the effects of coherence mainly on situation-level understanding). The writer's judgment of relevance is also subject to the bias in item 2.
5. **Assuming a senior-engineer reader to justify leaving out links.** The benefit of gappy text for knowledgeable readers is narrow: it appeared for less skilled high-knowledge readers, depended on reading order, and reversed for skilled high-knowledge readers (McNamara 2001; O'Reilly & McNamara 2007; Ozuru, Dempsey & McNamara 2009). It also requires that the reader already knows the missing link, which no reader can for session-specific facts such as tool output.
6. **Arrows, dashes and symbols in place of verbs.** Source: ASD-STE100 Rule 4.2 ("Do not omit verbs"); W3C COGA pattern 4.4.6 on unfamiliar symbols.
7. **Measuring a style rule by how easy replies feel to skim.** The studies above found that easier-feeling or faster-to-read text can be understood less well (Martin & Herndon 1972; Moraru, Biswas & Gadiraju 2026).

The evidence does not argue against answer-first structure, against cutting hedges, boilerplate and repeated summaries, or against short paragraphs. GOV.UK, W3C COGA and Anthropic's own guidance support those practices, and the verbosity research shows that padding is a real problem (Borisov et al. 2026; Singhal et al. 2023).

## 5. Claims that could not be verified

- The Hinds (1999) task details, such as the mobile-phone example, come from a secondary blog post; only the abstract was read.
- The phrases "much increased" recall and "more learning per unit of study time" for Britton & Gülgöz (1991) come from a search-engine snippet of the publisher abstract; only the ERIC summary was opened.
- Pinker's *The Sense of Style*, chapter 3, was not opened; the quotations come from his 2014 essay, read in an authorized reprint.
- Newton (1990) reports two different success counts (3 of 120 in the results and 2 of 150 in the abstract).
- Perfetti et al. (1987) on headlines and Millis & Just (1994) on connectives were not opened, so no findings from them are claimed.
- The GOV.UK claim that specialists prefer plain English cites research that GOV.UK does not name, and that research was not identified.
- The Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders (2019) article is open access, but only its abstract was read.
